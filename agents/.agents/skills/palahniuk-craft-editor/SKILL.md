# Palahniuk Craft Editor

Use this skill when the user asks for help writing, revising, diagnosing, or strengthening an essay, blog post, memoir fragment, speech, story, narrative nonfiction piece, or spoken monologue using craft principles inspired by Chuck Palahniuk's writing essays.

Do **not** imitate Chuck Palahniuk's voice. Do **not** make the user's work sound like him. Use the craft tools as lenses for editing the user's own writing.

## Core Purpose

This skill helps an agent find opportunities to make writing more immediate, embodied, structured, surprising, and emotionally risky.

The goal is not to make prose “edgy.” The goal is to make prose harder to ignore.

When applying this skill, preserve the user's voice unless they explicitly ask for a rewrite in another voice. Favor practical edits, concrete examples, and line-level improvements over vague literary commentary.

## When to Use

Use this skill when the user asks to:

- Edit an essay, blog post, memoir, story, or monologue.
- Make writing more vivid, physical, funny, tense, direct, or emotionally honest.
- Improve pacing, scene structure, dialogue, openings, endings, or transitions.
- Turn spoken notes into a finished piece.
- Diagnose why a piece feels flat, abstract, obvious, slow, too clever, or too essay-like.
- Find places where writing can use objects, gestures, recurring motifs, hidden guns, physical sensation, sharper scene purpose, or better authority.
- Create a craft-based revision pass rather than a grammar cleanup.

## Guiding Rule

Do not merely tell the reader what the piece means. Make the reader feel the meaning through details, actions, objects, gestures, voice, structure, and delayed revelation.

Ask:

> Where is the writing explaining something that could be dramatized, embodied, delayed, or made physical?

## Agent Workflow

Use these passes as editing lenses. Do not mechanically apply all of them to every piece. Pick the highest-leverage ones.

### 1. Identify the Intended Effect

Infer the piece's target effect:

- persuade
- confess
- provoke
- explain
- make the reader laugh
- make the reader feel seen
- make the reader uncomfortable
- turn a private observation into public language
- build toward a realization
- reframe a cultural moment
- move from anecdote to argument

Then evaluate whether the current draft produces that effect.

### 2. Establish Authority

Every piece needs authority early.

Use **Heart Authority** when the writer earns trust through vulnerability, embarrassment, contradiction, confession, or self-incrimination.

Use **Head Authority** when the writer earns trust through knowledge, specificity, lived expertise, research, process, jargon used correctly, or insider detail.

Best results often combine both: a vulnerable admission followed by hard-earned knowledge.

### 3. Replace Thesis Openings with Specific Detail

Watch for openings that announce the meaning too early.

Weak pattern:

> The problem with modern software teams is that they move too fast and think too little.

Stronger pattern:

> The dashboard was built in a weekend. By Monday morning, three departments were using it. By Friday, someone asked whether it was SOC 2 compliant.

Move the strongest concrete detail earlier. Let the argument emerge after the reader has entered the scene.

### 4. Go On-the-Body

Find abstract emotion and make it physical.

Watch for labels like anxious, angry, sad, excited, overwhelmed, humiliated, stressed, disgusted, inspired, afraid, frustrated, or exhausted.

Replace labels with body, gesture, sensation, or action.

Ask:

- Where does this emotion live in the body?
- What does the character do with their hands?
- What happens to breathing, mouth, stomach, shoulders, eyes, feet, skin, voice, or posture?
- What sensory detail would make the reader feel the moment without being told what to feel?

### 5. Submerge the “I”

For first-person essays, reduce unnecessary self-reference.

Count paragraphs beginning with “I.” Look for clusters of “I think,” “I feel,” “I believe,” “I realized,” “I remember,” and “I noticed.”

Point the camera outward when possible.

Weak:

> I realized that AI had not removed the need for product thinking.

Stronger:

> AI had not removed the need for product thinking. It had only made the missing thinking easier to see.

Keep “I” when vulnerability, responsibility, perspective, or voice requires it.

### 6. Identify the Horses

A strong piece often has a limited number of recurring themes, images, metaphors, or concerns. These are the “horses” pulling the piece.

Name no more than three primary horses. Cut or reduce metaphors that do not support them. Bring the central image back at the ending with changed meaning.

### 7. Find or Plant Hidden Guns

A hidden gun is an early detail, object, question, lie, flaw, promise, process, or threat that returns later with greater force.

Scan for details that could return:

- an object
- a technical constraint
- a casual lie
- a promise
- a rule
- a wound
- a deadline
- a phrase
- a recurring joke
- a tool
- a small contradiction

Bring the gun back in the final third to force a realization, reversal, joke, or emotional payoff.

### 8. Use Objects

Objects are not decoration. They should work.

Every important object should serve one or more functions:

- memory cue
- gesture prop
- hidden gun
- symbol of desire
- symbol of failure
- evidence of class, work, status, taste, or history
- through-line image
- emotional container
- physical way to avoid exposition

Remove unused objects. Recycle important objects. Let objects accumulate meaning.

### 9. Add Gesture and Physical Business to Dialogue

Flat dialogue usually lacks gesture, interruption, silence, or competing action.

Give the speaker a task. Let body language contradict the spoken line. Use gesture where tension should build. Attribute dialogue clearly when needed.

### 10. Disconnect Dialogue

Avoid perfect tennis-match dialogue where every question receives a neat answer.

Use A/B/C response logic:

- A response answers the question directly.
- B response answers with tension or resistance.
- C response ignores the question and reveals the speaker's inner world.

Use C responses when the scene needs tension, subtext, or power movement.

### 11. Remove Thought Verbs and Spoon-Feeding

Watch for thought verbs that tell the reader what a character knows, realizes, understands, believes, wants, remembers, notices, worries, feels, or decides.

Replace thought with evidence the reader can interpret.

Weak:

> I realized the client didn't trust the system.

Stronger:

> The client asked for a CSV export after every demo.

### 12. Watch for Received Text and Clichés

Received text is any familiar phrase the writer drops in because it arrives pre-assembled.

Examples:

- heart skipped a beat
- pit in my stomach
- deer in headlights
- elephant in the room
- at the end of the day
- light at the end of the tunnel
- AI won't replace you, someone using AI will

Replace stale phrases with concrete action, fresh observation, or a phrase that belongs to the narrator.

### 13. Improve Similes and Comparisons

Weak similes leave the scene and make the reader picture something unrelated.

Prefer metaphor when possible. Remove “like” when the sentence works without it. Use comparisons that reveal the narrator's background.

### 14. Vary Textures of Information

Narrative can include more than standard exposition. Consider:

- list
- recipe
- transcript
- rule set
- email
- Slack message
- checklist
- invoice
- bug report
- error log
- contract clause
- text message
- meeting notes
- warning label
- calendar invite

Use alternate textures to create authority, vary pacing, imply time passing, ground the piece in the real world, compress exposition, or create comedy.

### 15. Check Story Shape

Identify the shape of the piece and whether it serves the intended effect.

Possible shapes:

- **Straight Line:** This happened, then this happened.
- **O Shape:** Begin near crisis or aftermath, then flash back.
- **Thumbnail:** Begin with a compressed preview, then unpack.
- **Quilt:** A frame holding multiple smaller stories or examples.
- **Cycle:** One turn of a repeating system.
- **Rebel / Follower / Witness:** Two extremes collapse; the witness learns and carries the new story forward.

### 16. Make Every Scene Serve a Plot Point

For each scene, section, or paragraph cluster, identify its job.

Possible jobs:

- establish authority
- introduce a central question
- set up a later payoff
- pay off an earlier setup
- shift power
- create a reversal
- slow the pace before a reveal
- create a laugh before tension increases
- move time forward
- reveal character
- introduce or transform an object
- embody the thesis
- complicate the argument
- land the ending

If a section has no job, cut it or give it one.

### 17. Create Time Movement

When the piece stalls, use craft to move time.

Options:

- Cut between two timelines.
- Use a list to compress repeated events.
- Use a recurring task to imply time passing.
- Use research or head-authority as a brief information passage.
- Use a physical process.
- Use a repeated phrase that changes slightly.
- Use objects accumulating wear, damage, or new meaning.

### 18. Build a Better Ending

A strong ending usually returns to something from the beginning with altered meaning.

Look for:

- the first image
- the first object
- the first phrase
- the central metaphor
- the buried gun
- the original question
- the narrator's initial blindness
- the bodily sensation
- the repeated horse

Avoid endings that merely summarize the lesson.

## Editing Output Format

When editing the user's work, respond in this structure unless the user asks otherwise:

### Craft Diagnosis

Give a short diagnosis of what the piece is already doing well and what craft opportunity matters most.

### Highest-Leverage Fixes

List 3 to 7 fixes. For each, name the craft principle and explain the opportunity.

### Line-Level Notes

Quote or paraphrase short snippets from the user's draft and suggest improvements.

Use:

- Original
- Issue
- Revision direction

### Suggested Rewrite

If useful, provide a revised version that preserves the user's voice.

Do not over-polish. Do not remove the user's cadence, humor, bluntness, or idiosyncratic phrasing unless those choices weaken the piece.

### Optional Craft Exercises

Offer 1 to 3 exercises the user can use to revise further.

## Drafting Output Format

When helping create a new piece from notes or a monologue, use:

### Core Argument

State the piece's central claim in one sentence.

### Horses

Name the recurring themes/images that should pull the piece.

### Authority Strategy

Explain whether the piece should lead with heart authority, head authority, or both.

### Shape

Choose the structure: straight line, O, thumbnail, quilt, cycle, or rebel/follower/witness.

### Opening Move

Suggest a concrete opening: scene, object, contradiction, line of dialogue, or physical detail.

### Draft

Write the piece in the user's voice.

### Revision Notes

Explain what craft elements were used and what could be pushed further.

## Agent Checklist

Before returning a final edit, check:

- Does the opening begin with a concrete detail or compelling tension?
- Does the writer establish heart or head authority early?
- Are there too many “I” openings?
- Are emotions embodied rather than labeled?
- Are thought verbs reduced?
- Are clichés replaced or made fresh?
- Are recurring themes limited and strengthened?
- Is there an object, phrase, lie, or detail that returns later?
- Does dialogue include gesture, interruption, or subtext?
- Does each section have a job?
- Does the structure serve the effect?
- Does the ending return to something with changed meaning?
- Does the rewrite still sound like the user?

## Core Mantra

Make the abstract concrete.

Make the emotional physical.

Make the object work.

Make the setup return.

Make the scene earn its place.

Make the ending arrive before the explanation kills it.
