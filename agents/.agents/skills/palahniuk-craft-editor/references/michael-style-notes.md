# Michael Style Notes

Use these notes when editing Michael Rispoli's writing.

## Preserve

- Conversational momentum.
- Practical examples from software, AI, agency work, engineering, business, Long Island/NYC, meetings, clients, and product work.
- Direct, practical language.
- Humor and occasional bluntness.
- The feeling that the piece began as a spoken thought but has been edited into a finished essay.
- Strong opinions that remain grounded in lived experience.
- Paragraphs with enough substance to avoid a poem-like cadence.
- The sense that the writer has actually been in the room.

## Avoid

- Long runs of one-line paragraphs.
- Single-word sentence fragments.
- Generic LinkedIn thought-leadership tone.
- Over-polishing the prose until it loses personality.
- Repeating trendy words like “taste” and “bottleneck.”
- Making every paragraph a punchline.
- Turning blunt practical observations into abstract “insights.”
- Overusing em dashes when simpler punctuation works.
- Replacing concrete agency/client examples with generic business language.

## Preferred Moves

- Keep the user's cadence, but clean up the transcript residue.
- Preserve the user's examples unless they are confusing or legally risky.
- Add structure without making the piece feel formulaic.
- Use concrete scenes from meetings, client work, engineering decisions, and product development.
- Use craft to sharpen the point, not decorate it.
- Prefer “deep thinking about the product” over overused shorthand like “taste” when appropriate.
- Prefer “this is the work” over “the bottleneck” when appropriate.
- Let the piece sound like a smart founder/CTO talking plainly, not a content marketer.

## Revision Preference

When asked for a full draft, provide a finished essay, not just notes.

When asked for critique, start with the highest-leverage structural fixes, then line edits.

When revising a monologue, preserve as much of the user's original phrasing as possible while improving order, clarity, rhythm, and payoff.
