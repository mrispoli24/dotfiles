# Essay Digests

These are agent-usable summaries of the craft essays. They are not replacements for the source essays and should not be used to imitate the author's prose style.

## 1. Establishing Your Authority

A story or essay needs authority early. Authority can come from vulnerability, where the narrator risks looking foolish or ashamed, or from knowledge, where the narrator demonstrates expertise, research, and specificity. Heart authority creates emotional trust. Head authority creates intellectual trust. Avoid relying on cleverness, charm, or bullying the reader with conclusions.

Agent move: Find the moment where the writer either risks honesty or demonstrates earned knowledge. Move that moment earlier or sharpen it.

## 2. Developing a Theme

Limit the number of themes or “horses.” A piece gains depth when a few motifs recur in different forms. A collage of details can work if all the details steer the reader toward the same emotional conclusion.

Agent move: Identify the 1–3 horses pulling the piece. Cut competing metaphors and return to the strongest one later with changed meaning.

## 3. Using “On-the-Body” Physical Sensation

Do not label sensation with abstract pain or emotion words. Unpack the event detail by detail so the reader experiences it physically. Strong writing engages mind, heart, and body.

Agent move: Replace abstract feeling with body, gesture, texture, smell, pressure, temperature, breath, mouth, hands, feet, or posture.

## 4. Submerging the “I”

First person can create authority, but too many “I” statements make the prose feel like boasting or self-absorption. Keep the camera pointed away from the narrator when possible. Let objects, actions, other people, and scenes carry the story.

Agent move: Reduce “I think,” “I realized,” “I remember,” and “I noticed.” Recast sentences so the insight stands on its own.

## 5. Nuts and Bolts: Hiding a Gun

Introduce a detail, question, clock, process, object, or threat early, let the reader forget it, then bring it back when the story needs crisis or resolution. A hidden gun can be obvious or subtle, but it must feel earned.

Agent move: Scan the opening for unused details. Bring one back in the final third.

## 6. Beware the “Thesis Statement”

A thesis-style opening explains the point before the reader cares. It kills intrigue. Start with a specific, tangible detail that lets the reader infer the point. Unpack the detail before naming the meaning.

Agent move: Cut abstract first paragraphs. Start with the strongest concrete image, scene, action, or contradiction.

## 7. Reading Out Loud — Part One

Reading aloud reveals timing, clunky sentences, vague patches, missing attribution, weak jokes, overlong lines, and moments that need pause. Writing is performance. Sound matters. Verbs matter.

Agent move: Encourage a read-aloud pass. Look for breath, rhythm, hard consonants, laugh points, tension breaks, and places where the prose becomes hard to say.

## 8. Reading Out Loud — Part Two

Storytelling is how people make sense of life. Creative work transforms private, unresolved material into crafted public form. Time should be described through character-specific perception rather than abstract units when possible.

Agent move: Ask how this narrator measures time. Replace generic time markers with personal, concrete measures.

## 9. Nuts and Bolts: Punctuation with Gesture and Attribution

Dialogue needs gesture, physical business, and attribution. Gesture can pace a conversation better than punctuation. People rarely stand still and exchange lines.

Agent move: Insert action beats that control timing, reveal subtext, and orient the reader.

## 10. When You Can’t Find a Writing Workshop

Writers often write better when “trapped” in structured, boring, public settings. Workshops also provide permission, ritual, observation, and audience. Stories are everywhere if the writer listens.

Agent move: For blocked drafting, prescribe a writing trap: public, boring, structured, low-distraction, with a notebook. For essays, harvest real voices and details from the world.

## 11. Learning from the Cliches... Then Leaving Them Behind

Clichés and “received text” are shortcuts that prevent fresh perception. The writer's job is to reinvent familiar actions through the character's body, history, and language. Mimicking admired writers can be useful training, but clichés must be rejected.

Agent move: Flag stale phrases. Replace them with specific, character-rooted observation.

## 12. Talking Shapes: The Quilt Versus the Big O

The O shape begins near the crisis or aftermath and then flashes back. The Quilt shape uses a frame to hold multiple stories or fragments. Both avoid a plain “and then, and then” line.

Agent move: Choose O when a strong crisis can hook the reader. Choose Quilt when multiple examples or anecdotes need a frame.

## 13. Textures of Information

Stories can borrow real-world information forms: lists, recipes, rules, labels, graffiti, prayers, ads, contracts, reports, warnings, directions, slogans, transcripts, and more. Texture varies pacing and borrows authority from the form.

Agent move: Add a borrowed format only when it advances story, compresses exposition, or deepens authority.

## 14. Effective Similes

Similes often weaken prose by pulling the reader out of the scene. Metaphor is usually stronger. If using comparison, avoid lazy “like” and “is” constructions. Use comparisons that reveal the narrator's world.

Agent move: Strike weak “like” comparisons. Convert to metaphor, action, or character-specific comparison.

## 15. Talking Shapes: The Thumbnail

A thumbnail opening previews the plot or argument in miniature. It can create authority, signal that big things will happen, and make later developments feel inevitable. It should be grounded in a tangible scene.

Agent move: For sprawling pieces, write a compact opening overture that previews the whole without explaining everything.

## 16. Talking Shapes: The Cycle

Cycle stories show one repetition of a larger system: sanctuary, seduction, denial, deaths or losses, entrapment, discovery, escape attempt, aftermath, and next victim. The power comes from realizing the process has happened before and will happen again.

Agent move: Use cycle shape for essays about systems, incentives, institutions, recurring business failure, or cultural traps.

## 17. Talking Shapes: The Rebel, the Follower and the Witness

Many stories contain three roles: the rebel who resists, the follower who conforms, and the witness who survives to understand. The witness often becomes the narrator or transformed adult.

Agent move: Map the draft's people or ideas onto rebel/follower/witness. Use the witness position to create nuance beyond binary conflict.

## 18. Nuts and Bolts: Using Your Objects

Objects can function as memory cues, hidden guns, gesture props, and through-line images. Reusing objects gives them power. Objects should accrue meaning each time they appear.

Agent move: Inventory objects. Remove clutter. Recycle the strongest object and let it change meaning.

## 19. Thirteen Writing Tips

Use timers and chores to create writing rhythm. Know a scene's purpose before writing it. Surprise yourself. Return to earlier scenes to find buried guns. Spend time with other writers. Stay with not knowing. Change names to create distance. Mix descriptive, instructive, and expressive speech. Write about what truly upsets you.

Agent move: Use as a process checklist when the user is blocked or when a draft lacks scene purpose.

## 20. Killing Time — Part One

Fiction condenses time. Time can pass through signposts, parallel plots, information dumps, big voice, bodily sensation, and recurring actions. Information passages can build authority, reveal state of mind, vary texture, and move time forward.

Agent move: Replace dead transitions with physical processes, lists, research, or repeated action.

## 21. Disconnected Dialogue — Part One

Perfect question-answer exchanges flatten tension. Characters can ask questions that go unanswered. Responses can dodge, resist, or reveal a hidden agenda.

Agent move: Convert some direct answers into B or C choices. Let questions hang.

## 22. Body Language — Part One

Much communication happens through posture, gesture, movement, and physical behavior. Watching films on mute can build an inventory of gesture. Movement keeps scenes alive.

Agent move: Add body language that contradicts, reinforces, or complicates speech.

## 23. Objects

A good object can shift meaning over time: joke, love token, memory, betrayal, power, burden, contract, or proof. Limited objects focus energy.

Agent move: Make the key object morph. Ask what the object means at first, middle, and end.

## 24. Required Reading: Absurdity

Absurdity works when it is fast, concrete, specific, and authoritative. Enter quickly, overwhelm with detail, let perspective warp, and get out before the energy dies.

Agent move: For comic or absurd pieces, intensify concrete specificity and accelerate pacing.

## 25. Utility Phrases: When All Words Fail

Characters need phrases for moments when language fails. These stock phrases can reveal personality, bridge silence, create rhythm, and return as a chorus.

Agent move: Give characters or narrators signature utility phrases. Reuse them with changed meaning.

## 26. Names Versus Pronouns

Pronouns are efficient but vague. People and objects can be referred to by appearance, relationship, role, nickname, title, or changing identity. Names are often less memorable than action or relationship.

Agent move: Replace some pronouns with labels that reveal attitude, relationship, or transformation. Avoid confusing the reader.

## 27. Nuts and Bolts: Plot Points

Every scene or passage should have a purpose: setup, payoff, reversal, lull, reveal, question, or power shift. Do not write scenes hoping the point will reveal itself.

Agent move: Label the job of each section. Cut or revise sections with no job.

## 28. Tell a Lie, Bury a Gun

A lie, secret, crime, or insincere promise can function as a buried gun. The lie gives power; revelation reverses power. Truth should force crisis or reconnection.

Agent move: Identify the lie or self-deception. Plant it early and force it into the open.

## 29. A Story from Scratch, Act One

Begin with physical action. Establish setting through movement. Submerge first person. Introduce rivalry, activity, important objects, and a first plot point that raises questions.

Agent move: For narrative drafting, start with action and end Act One on a turn.

## 30. A Story from Scratch, Act Two

Escalate the quest. Use big voice to vary texture and imply time passing. Keep objects in motion. Push the character toward an ethical choice. First drafts often build the horizontal plot before the vertical emotion.

Agent move: Track both horizontal events and vertical emotional accumulation.

## 31. A Story from Scratch, Act Three

Pay off earlier objects, gestures, and rivalries. Keep bodily details continuous. Resolve the social contract. After the horizontal is complete, revise for the vertical: emotion, symbolism, transformation.

Agent move: Make the ending pay off the opening and show transformation through action, not explanation.
